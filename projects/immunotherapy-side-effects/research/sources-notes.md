# Source notes: What to expect from immunotherapy side effects
Searched 2026-10-09 by Claude. Keys refer to research/sources.md.

## What is a checkpoint inhibitor and why does it cause side effects?
- Checkpoints are normal "brakes" on the immune system that stop T cells from harming healthy cells; some tumors use them to switch T cells off, and checkpoint inhibitors block that off signal so T cells can attack the cancer [S1]
- The drug targets are CTLA-4, PD-1, and PD-L1, and newer LAG-3; PD-1 drugs include pembrolizumab, nivolumab, cemiplimab; PD-L1 drugs include atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab; CTLA-4 drugs ipilimumab and tremelimumab are usually combined with a PD-1 or PD-L1 drug [S4]
- Most are given by IV infusion; some can be given as an injection under the skin [S4]
- Because the drugs remove a safeguard, the immune system may attack healthy parts of the body, which is what causes the side effects [S4]
- Side effects happen when the boosted immune system also attacks healthy cells and tissues [S2]
- These immune-related side effects differ from chemotherapy side effects because immunotherapy works differently [S6]

## Which side effects are common?
- Common: rash, diarrhea, and fatigue [S1]
- Common list from ACS: diarrhea, fatigue, cough, nausea and vomiting, skin rash, poor appetite, constipation, muscle and joint pain [S4]
- Common list from CRUK: skin rashes and dry itchy skin, diarrhoea, tiredness, nausea, loss of appetite, breathlessness and dry cough from lung inflammation [S8]
- Feeling tired is common during immunotherapy [S6]
- Skin reactions and fatigue are the most common; many of the skin side effects are common and very few are serious [S5]
- Diarrhea and inflamed bowel symptoms are common lower GI side effects [S5]
- Some people have mild reactions during the infusion itself: low-grade fever, chills, headache, nausea; medicine may be given before the infusion to prevent severe reactions [S5]
- Infusion reactions can include fever, chills, facial flushing, rash, itching, dizziness, wheezing, trouble breathing; tell the nurse right away [S4]
- In one melanoma study of 387 people on nivolumab or pembrolizumab after surgery, 69 percent had an immune-related side effect during treatment, and 87 percent of those were mild [S3]
- More than 60 percent of patients receiving some form of immunotherapy develop side effects [S7]
- You are unlikely to get all of the side effects your team lists [S9]
- You are more likely to have side effects if you take more than one kind of immunotherapy or immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy [S5]

## Which side effects are serious or rare?
- Rarer side effects: widespread inflammation of the skin, lungs, colon, pancreas (diabetes), liver (hepatitis), pituitary gland, heart muscle (myocarditis), kidneys, thyroid, or nerves [S1]
- Autoimmune reactions can cause serious or life-threatening problems in the lungs, intestines, liver, hormone-making glands, kidneys, or other organs [S4]
- Many side effects are mild and can be treated, but some rare and serious ones can lead to death, especially if untreated [S5]
- Severe or fatal inflammation-related reactions are rare [S2]
- Heart inflammation (myocarditis) is rare but can come on quickly and become very severe [S5]
- Hyperinflammation syndromes are a rare but very serious side effect [S5]
- Hypophysitis (inflamed pituitary) is rare; it can cause adrenal insufficiency and may need hydrocortisone for life [S5]
- Diabetes can develop because of ICI treatment, although rare, and may need to be managed for life [S5]
- Label numbers for pembrolizumab in 2,799 patients: pneumonitis 3.4 percent, colitis 1.7 percent, hepatitis 0.7 percent, hypothyroidism 8 percent, hyperthyroidism 3.4 percent, adrenal insufficiency 0.8 percent, hypophysitis 0.6 percent, type 1 diabetes 0.2 percent, nephritis 0.3 percent, severe skin reactions 1.4 percent [S10]

## When do side effects start, and how long do they last?
- They may start shortly after you begin treatment, within the first couple of months, or even after treatment is finished [S5]
- Skin symptoms usually start in the first several weeks of treatment [S5]
- Diarrhea and inflamed bowel symptoms often start in the first 6 to 8 weeks of treatment [S5]
- Most side effects appear within the first few weeks or months; rarely they appear months or years later [S7]
- Side effects can occur at any point during or after treatment [S2]
- Immune-related side effects can start right away or up to 2 years after treatment ends [S11]
- Side effects can begin during treatment or weeks, months, or more than a year after it ends [S9]
- Side effects can occur during treatment or several months after it has finished [S8]
- Label: immune-mediated reactions can occur at any time after starting treatment and after treatment stops [S10]
- Some side effects are chronic and may need to be managed for the rest of your life [S5]
- In the melanoma study, 43 percent had an immune-related side effect lasting at least 3 months after finishing treatment; 96 percent of those were mild; the most common were rash, underactive thyroid, and joint pain; the ones most likely to persist were adrenal insufficiency, joint pain, eye problems, pituitary inflammation, and thyroid problems [S3]
- GI symptoms often return to baseline in two to four weeks with treatment [S7]
- Thyroid levels may return to normal after 4 to 6 weeks, or may drop too low (hypothyroidism), which is treated with levothyroxine [S5]

## What should I watch for and when should I call?
- Reporting symptoms when they start helps find the cause early and helps prevent severe side effects [S6]
- Steroids work best when started early, when symptoms first come up; if you notice new or worsening symptoms, talk to your care team right away [S5]
- Side effects can start mild and become serious without treatment; save the 24-hour hospital numbers and call any time you feel unwell [S9]
- Report any new side effect as soon as possible and mention any change in how you feel; early treatment can prevent problems from worsening [S4]
- MSK thresholds: call for more than 3 watery stools in a day, a change in diarrhea appearance, or blood in stool; fever of 100.4 F (38 C) or higher; call right away for new or worsening cough, shortness of breath, chest pain, yellowing skin or eyes, severe belly pain, blurry or double vision, persistent headaches, extreme tiredness, dizziness or fainting, confusion [S11]
- Do not take diarrhea medicine or laxatives without first talking to your provider [S11]
- Warning signs by body part: lungs (trouble breathing, dry cough, fever, chest pain); bowel (watery or loose stools, blood or mucus in stool, stomach pain); skin (rash, itching, blisters); thyroid (weight change, constipation, dry skin, feeling cold); pituitary (loss of appetite, nausea, dizziness); eyes (blurred vision, blind spots, eye pain or redness); heart (chest pain, irregular heartbeat, shortness of breath, weakness); nerves (muscle weakness, weakness in the face); brain (headaches, light sensitivity, confusion); joints (pain, swelling, stiffness) [S6]
- MD Anderson emergency list: shortness of breath at rest, chest pain or heartbeat changes, severe abdominal pain or more than seven bowel movements a day, blood or black tarry stools, vision loss, headache or vomiting that will not stop, sudden weakness or loss of feeling, skin blistering or peeling or rash over more than half the body [S7]
- Chest pain, chest tightness, or difficulty breathing needs an immediate emergency call [S9]
- Thyroid, liver, and kidney function are checked with blood tests before and during treatment [S10]
- Regular blood tests check the liver, kidneys, and hormone glands such as the thyroid [S8]

## How are side effects treated, and does treatment have to stop?
- Side effects are often treated with steroids, and treatment depends on how severe they are and how much they affect your life [S5]
- Many side effects are treated with steroids: prednisone by mouth, methylprednisolone by IV, creams for skin, eye drops for eyes [S5]
- Steroids can irritate the stomach, weaken bones over time, and raise infection risk; doses are lowered slowly (tapered) because stopping too quickly can bring the side effect back [S5]
- Not all side effects are managed with steroids; other treatments include biologics such as TNF blockers, IVIG, hormone replacement, and other immune blockers [S5]
- In some cases the first step is to pause or stop the immunotherapy, which usually stops the side effect too [S5]
- Serious side effects may require stopping treatment and high-dose corticosteroids [S4]
- Label rule in general: withhold for Grade 3 (severe) immune-mediated reactions; permanently discontinue for Grade 4 (life-threatening) [S10]
- If treatment was paused for a severe side effect, the care team weighs how well the cancer responded and the risks and benefits before restarting, and discusses it with you [S5]
- Resuming checkpoint inhibitors after colitis is described as standard care; the stated goal is to reduce medications quickly so treatment can resume [S7]
- Hormone problems are treated with replacement: hydrocortisone for cortisol, levothyroxine for thyroid, insulin for diabetes [S5]
- Tell the team before starting any new medicine, including steroids, over-the-counter drugs, vitamins, supplements, or herbal remedies [S11]

## Does a side effect mean the drug is working?
- Side effects do not necessarily mean treatment is not working; thyroid dysfunction and colitis are associated with better survival and response in some studies [S7]
- Clinicians cannot reliably predict when or whether side effects will occur or how severe they will be [S1]

## Practical things
- Ask the care team for an immunotherapy wallet card listing your regimen, possible side effects, and contact numbers; carry it at all times [S5]
- Keep the alert card with you during and after treatment and show it to any doctor or health professional [S9]
- Tell your primary care team and all health professionals that you are having or have had ICI treatment [S5]
- Use birth control during immunotherapy and for at least 5 months after the last dose; the same for breastfeeding [S5]
- Inactive or killed vaccines are safe during ICI treatment [S5]
- Fatigue can be caused by thyroid changes, which the care team will check [S11]

## Conflicts
- Frequency of any side effect: S3 reports 69 percent in one adjuvant melanoma cohort, S7 says more than 60 percent for "some form of immunotherapy", and S10 label percentages are per-organ for one drug. These are not the same measure; the script should use S3 for the cohort number and S10 for organ-level rates, and not blend them.
- How late side effects can start: S11 says up to 2 years after treatment ends; S9 says more than a year; S8 says several months; S7 says rarely months or years later. All agree they can start after treatment stops; the script should say "months, and in rare cases a year or more" and cite S9 and S11.
